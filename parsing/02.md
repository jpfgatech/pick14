# Parsing: Match-count regression (instructions/02.md)

## Understanding

The question is whether the number of matches a player makes within a game is the
dominant driver of their final score — independent of *which* cards they matched.

This matters strategically: if R² is high, a greedy "make as many matches as
possible" heuristic may be near-optimal.  If R² is low, card selection (which
cards to match and when) matters more.

---

## Three regression variants

Let `M_i` = matches made by player i, `S_i` = score points by player i, all in one
game.  `avg(·)` is the within-game mean over all players.

| Name | x | y | What it measures |
|------|---|---|-----------------|
| **Raw** | M_i | S_i | Absolute match count → absolute score (includes cross-game variance) |
| **Raw-var** | M_i | S_i − avg(S) | Absolute match count → score advantage |
| **Var** | M_i − avg(M) | S_i − avg(S) | Relative match count → relative score advantage (cleanest) |

All three use OLS (y = a + b·x).  The Var regression is the key one:
its slope tells how many extra score points one extra match above the game average is
worth, and its R² tells whether match count fully explains who wins within a game.

---

## Simulation plan

- 10 000 games each for 2p, 3p, 4p (GFP match + caution play, n_hand=3)
- Track per player per game: `n_matches`, `final_score`
- Flatten across players and games → one (x, y) pair per (game, player)

## Expected outcomes

1. **Raw R² high**: absolute score is largely determined by how many matches you
   make (each match draws an extra card + captures points directly).
2. **Var R²** likely lower than raw but still meaningful: much of the raw correlation
   is shared across players in the same game; within-game variation from match count
   is real but noisier.
3. **Slope of Var regression** ≈ expected capture per match (~6–10 pts, mixing
   all suit combinations).
4. **Consistency across player counts**: the slopes should be roughly similar (the
   value of one extra match is approximately the same); R² may decrease with more
   players because variance is split among more competitors.
