## Motivation

We start with some aggregate analysis on the keys
E.g. group the keys by number of unique digits present in public card
E.g. group the keys by number of unique sums < 14 of hand card
- Joker, 5H, 9c counts as 2, because out of the 7 possible sums, only 5 and 9 are <14
- AC, 2C, 4C counts as seven because they can make 1/2/3/4/5/6/7 sums. They should create a huge number of match options


We now try to find ways that maximizes the probability of match, while tracking the point gap
Focusing on match number means we are ignoring match quality, i.e. points made
Part of the argument is that the points of a match is dominated by the cards suite
Which is not controlled by player anyhow
Therefore, in the current study we ignore the points as if cards has no suite
(This argument is not fully valid, since upon a match and play decision, the player knows the suite)
(But we shall proceed for now)

## Math Model

We take a snapshot of the game after every match or play action
the snapshot is denoted by an integer vector
there are 13 integers representing the presence of card digit in hand card
e.g. for Joker, 5 of HEART, and 9 of club, we have:
HAND_CARD[5-1] = 2, HAND_CARD[9-1] = 1, counting from zero
similarly for public pool

For each action, we denote the game outcome two rounds into the future

- [snapshot A] match or pass by player of round N
- (refill after match, if applicable)
- [snapshot B1, if applicable] play phase 1, passed, of round N
- (refill after play phase 1)
- [snapshot B2, if applicable] play phase 2, matched, of round N
- the other players' action phase, symmetric

the exact sequence might differ with current game phase definition
the key is that there are two types of snapshots A/B
and for B there are two variants, based on match or pass outcome

Then for each snapshot, record the outcome of the next two rounds as well:
- snapshotA[N]: number of available match options on turn N+1 and N+2, total points, as well as point gap against average of all players including oneself
- snapshotB1/2[N]: number of available match options on turn N+1 and N+2, total points, as well as point gap against average of all players including oneself

(current policy is that as long as a match option is available player will never pass)

keey turn N+1 and turn N+2 separable, so we can analyze for turn N+1 only as well

## Findings

Setup: same 20k-game database from 03-1, re-aggregated by (snap_type, pub_ud, hand_us).

### Grouping reduces state space to 235 H2 / 241 H1 buckets

- **pub_ud** (unique digit values in public pool): range 0–9 in practice
- **hand_us** (unique subset-sums < 14 in hand): range 1–13 for 3-card hands
- Most samples sit in pub_ud 2–6, hand_us 3–7

### Both features predict future match quality monotonically

From the H2 bucket table (snapshot type A — post-match/pass):

| hand_us | mopt (H2) | pts (H2) | gap (H2) |
|---------|-----------|----------|----------|
| 2 | ~1.8 | ~5.4 | −2.1 |
| 4 | ~2.7 | ~7.5 | −0.7 |
| 6 | ~3.8 | ~9.4 | +0.8 |
| 8 | ~5.4 | +11.2 | +2.4 |
| 11 | ~6.8 | +12.7 | +3.7 |

All four metrics (mopt, mcat, pts, gap) increase monotonically with hand_us, holding pub_ud fixed. The effect is strong and consistent across pub_ud values.

pub_ud shows a similar but somewhat weaker monotone trend — more distinct digit targets in the pool means more matching opportunity, but the hand richness (hand_us) is the dominant predictor.

### Gap sign change

Snapshots with hand_us ≤ 3 systematically produce **negative future gap** (below-average scoring), while hand_us ≥ 6 yields **positive gap**. The crossover occurs around hand_us ≈ 4–5.

This suggests that hand combinatorial richness (number of ways the hand can contribute to a 14-sum match) is the best single-number Q-state proxy currently available.

### Snapshot type comparison (A vs B1 vs B2)

B2 (post-play on a matched turn) shows slightly higher mopt and pts at equivalent feature values than B1 (post-play on a pass turn), consistent with the matched state having more remaining public cards to match against.

## Findings

Setup used: 20k games total as requested, split into two independent 10k batches (4-player baseline policy: greedy-for-public match + caution play).

### 1) Database density

- Distinct snapshot keys (H2 labels): **666,613**
- Samples/key: **p50=1**, **p90=2**, **p99=8**, **max=33**
- The two 10k runs align closely (top-1k average count: 9.13 vs 9.12), so coverage is stable despite sparse keys.

This confirms a very long-tail state space: most exact hash keys are rare, but aggregate density curves are reproducible.

### 2) Outcome scale (per-key means)

- **H2 (N+1..N+2 aggregate)**  
  `mopt` mean 4.09, `pts` mean 8.37, `gap` mean +1.49
- **H1 (N+1 only)**  
  `mopt` mean 2.02, `pts` mean 4.18, `gap` mean +1.56

As expected, H2 roughly doubles match-options and points vs H1; gap does not double because it is relative-to-average and can mean-revert.

### 3) Correlations among the three entries

Order: `[match options, points, point gap]`

- **H2 Pearson**
  - mopt↔pts: **0.729**
  - mopt↔gap: **0.425**
  - pts↔gap: **0.520**
- **H2 Spearman**
  - mopt↔pts: **0.809**
  - mopt↔gap: **0.452**
  - pts↔gap: **0.513**
- **H1 Pearson / Spearman**
  - mopt↔pts: **0.696 / 0.796**
  - mopt↔gap: **0.350 / 0.380**
  - pts↔gap: **0.403 / 0.398**

Interpretation:
- The strongest relation is **match options ↔ points** (both rank and raw), which supports “maximize future matchability” as a useful value signal.
- Correlation with **point gap** is moderate, not dominant: tactical matchability transfers to competitive lead, but with weaker and noisier mapping.
- H2 correlations are slightly stronger than H1, meaning one additional future turn adds useful signal rather than pure noise in this setup.
